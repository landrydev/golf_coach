## 1. Executive summary

The business is a Canada-wide, self-serve B2B SaaS product for individual independent golf instructors.

It helps an instructor sell more lesson packages, renew more golfers, and generate more referrals by turning information the instructor already has into a personalized, coach-branded development roadmap and living player journey.

The product is not a new coaching methodology. It sits above the instructor's current coaching tools, evidence, methods, and commercial links. It makes the plan clearer to the golfer and makes the next appropriate coaching commitment easier to understand.

The initial product and acquisition model must be:

- plug-and-play;
- understandable without a sales call;
- purchasable and cancellable without a custom quote or contract;
- configurable without mandatory onboarding;
- standardized enough to support many instructors;
- personal enough to preserve coach credibility;
- useful with existing booking, payment, communication, video, and launch-monitor tools; and
- economical at **[PRICING HYPOTHESIS — REQUIRES VALIDATION] CAD $75 per month per individual instructor**.

The first product wedge is:

> Assessment → personalized roadmap → recommended first coaching phase → lesson-package decision

The broader commercial loop is:

> Assessment → personalized roadmap → initial lesson-package purchase → visible progress → phase completion → next-package renewal → referral

The initial customer is one independent instructor, not a golf-course procurement team or enterprise account. Small academies and multi-coach facilities are later self-serve team-tier possibilities only after the solo model earns evidence.

## 3. Customer problem and business opportunity

### 3.1 Instructor problem

`[UNVALIDATED BUSINESS ASSUMPTION]` Many instructors can diagnose a golfer and coach effectively but struggle to turn an assessment or initial lesson into a clear, persuasive, long-term development plan. The golfer may receive advice, observations, videos, drills, or launch data without understanding:

- what matters most;
- why it matters to the golfer's stated goal;
- what should happen first;
- how the work fits into a sequence;
- what evidence will show progress; and
- why a lesson package is the appropriate next commitment.

The commercial problem is therefore not simply lead generation. It is the gap between coaching expertise and a golfer's confidence in a structured plan.

### 3.2 Golfer problem

`[UNVALIDATED BUSINESS ASSUMPTION]` Golfers may hesitate to buy a package because the future feels vague, generic, overly technical, or sales-led. A personalized roadmap can reduce uncertainty when it connects the golfer's goal, starting evidence, priority barriers, development phases, and next action in plain language.

### 3.3 Opportunity

The product creates a reusable presentation layer around information the instructor already uses. It should make professional coaching easier to understand and easier to continue, without claiming guaranteed performance outcomes.

## 4. Initial customer

### 4.1 Primary customer profile

`[SUPPORTED BY BUSINESS PLAN V2]` The initial customer is an independent golf instructor or teaching professional who:

- personally controls or influences how lesson packages are sold;
- conducts assessments, evaluations, or initial lessons;
- sells multiple lessons to at least some golfers;
- wants more golfers to commit to structured coaching;
- already has some combination of observations, videos, drills, measurements, or launch data;
- wants a more professional and persuasive golfer experience;
- does not want to replace current coaching tools or methodology;
- is comfortable using simple self-serve software; and
- could justify **[PRICING HYPOTHESIS — REQUIRES VALIDATION] approximately CAD $75 per month** from a small number of additional package sales or renewals.

### 4.2 Qualifying conditions

The strongest early candidate controls the package recommendation and has a repeatable assessment-to-offer moment. Facility type, employer type, launch-monitor brand, coaching philosophy, and region within Canada are not qualifying requirements.

### 4.3 Explicit non-requirements

The instructor does not need to:

- own a golf course;
- manage an academy or multiple coaches;
- work at a specific facility type;
- adopt proprietary coaching methodology;
- use a particular launch monitor;
- replace booking, payment, communication, or analysis tools; or
- purchase consulting, custom implementation, or concierge services.

### 4.4 Later segments

Small academies, indoor-golf businesses, and multi-coach facilities may later need shared branding, coach seats, administration, permissions, or aggregate reporting. Those needs are discovery inputs for a future self-serve team tier, not reasons to burden the initial solo product.

`[REAL-WORLD VALIDATION REQUIRED]` Evidence must determine whether any later segment deserves earlier attention. Until then, facility-level enterprise selling is out of the initial strategy.

## 5. Positioning and value proposition

### 5.1 Positioning

For independent golf instructors who want more golfers to commit to structured coaching, the product creates personalized, coach-branded development roadmaps from the instructor's existing assessment evidence. Unlike a generic lesson recap, a booking tool, or a custom design service, it helps the golfer understand the plan and the appropriate next package while preserving the instructor's methodology and current tools.

Candidate shorthand:

> Sell the plan, not another hour.

`[SIMULATED OWNER DECISION — REQUIRES AARON REVIEW]` Use that shorthand as a working message, paired with plain-language explanation so it is not mistaken for manipulative sales automation.

### 5.2 Value to the instructor

- Make coaching recommendations look clear, personal, and professional.
- Connect assessment evidence to a structured development sequence.
- Give the golfer a credible reason to choose a package rather than another isolated lesson.
- Make progress, phase completion, renewal, and referral moments visible over time.
- Reuse standardized structures without making every golfer feel generic.
- Preserve the instructor's brand, voice, methods, and existing commercial workflow.
- Reduce the need to assemble one-off recaps or explain the whole plan repeatedly.

### 5.3 Value to the golfer

- Understand the desired outcome and current starting point.
- See the few barriers that matter most without unnecessary technical overload.
- Know what will happen first and why.
- Understand how the recommended package supports the first phase.
- See progress evidence and what comes next.
- Feel invited to make a confident decision rather than pressured into a purchase.

## 6. Product thesis and commercial loop

### 6.1 Product thesis

The product curates the instructor's existing golfer goals, assessment findings, observations, swing videos, drills, measurements, lesson history, and progress evidence into a personalized, coach-branded roadmap and player journey.

The value comes from curation, sequencing, presentation, and continuity. It does not require the product to generate coaching diagnoses or replace the coach's judgment.

### 6.2 First wedge

The first product wedge must help the instructor move from an assessment to a shareable roadmap that contains:

1. the golfer's desired outcome;
2. the coach's concise starting assessment;
3. a small number of priority barriers;
4. a small number of development phases;
5. a clearly recommended first phase;
6. the relevant lesson package; and
7. the instructor's existing purchase, booking, or contact destination.

### 6.3 Living journey

After the first package decision, the same narrative can later support lesson history, progress evidence, milestone recognition, phase completion, next-phase recommendation, renewal, and referral. These later moments are part of the product thesis but must not expand the first activation loop beyond what can be designed and validated coherently.

## 7. Recommended first product scope

`[SIMULATED OWNER DECISION — REQUIRES AARON REVIEW]` The first self-serve product should enable one instructor to:

1. create an instructor account;
2. enter basic coach identity and branding;
3. define one or more current lesson-package recommendations;
4. create a minimal golfer profile;
5. record the golfer's desired outcome;
6. record a concise starting assessment;
7. identify the most important barriers;
8. define three or four development phases from standardized structures;
9. recommend the first phase;
10. connect that phase to a lesson package;
11. preview the golfer-facing experience;
12. share the roadmap;
13. direct the golfer to an existing purchase, booking, or contact link; and
14. return later to find and update the roadmap through a minimal product state.

The initial scope needs only enough account and return navigation to complete this loop. It does not require a complete coach dashboard or academy administration system.

### 7.1 Standardization rule

The product should provide standardized prompts, examples, phase structures, plain-language guidance, and templates. The coach supplies the diagnosis, priorities, wording choices, package, and evidence. Optional fields should never block first value.

### 7.2 Product exclusions

The initial product is not:

- a booking platform;
- a payment processor;
- a general CRM;
- a swing-analysis engine;
- a communication platform;
- a public coach marketplace;
- a launch-monitor replacement;
- a complete academy management system;
- a custom website builder;
- an autonomous coaching system;
- a custom branding agency; or
- a managed reporting service.

The instructor may paste an external link for booking, purchase, or contact. No integration is required to prove the first wedge.

## 12. Seasonality policy

### 12.1 Options considered

| Option | Customer benefit | Business benefit | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cancel and reactivate | Maximum simplicity and no off-season charge | Clean price story | Data-retention uncertainty and avoidable reactivation friction |
| Seasonal pause | Keeps account and data ready | Preserves relationship and creates reactivation signal | Adds one policy and a small price decision |
| Reduced-cost read-only access | Maintains reference access | Off-season revenue | May feel like paying for inactivity |
| Discounted annual billing | Predictability and simple renewal | Better cash flow and lower visible churn | Can deter seasonal instructors and obscure real retention |
| Monthly plus optional annual | Choice for seasonal and year-round coaches | Can support both behaviors | More packaging complexity before evidence |

### 12.2 Recommended initial policy

`[SIMULATED OWNER DECISION — REQUIRES AARON REVIEW]` Launch with month-to-month service at **[PRICING HYPOTHESIS — REQUIRES VALIDATION] CAD $75 per month**, cancel anytime, plus an optional seasonal pause at **[PRICING HYPOTHESIS — REQUIRES VALIDATION] CAD $15 per month** for up to six consecutive months. Pause should preserve the instructor's account, branding, package setup, and prior roadmaps in read-only form, with one clear action to resume full use. Defer annual billing until actual retention and seasonal behavior are known.

This recommendation balances customer control, data continuity, reactivation friction, and some contribution to retention costs. The pause price, maximum duration, data treatment, reminder cadence, and eligibility all require validation and appropriate privacy/legal review. Indoor and year-round instructors can remain on ordinary monthly service.

Forced annual contracts are not recommended for initial launch because they could suppress adoption and hide rather than solve seasonal churn.

## 13. Business model and unit economics

### 13.1 Revenue model

Primary revenue is recurring solo-instructor subscription revenue. Later team-tier expansion is optional and must not be required for the solo economics to work.

Core formulas:

- `Monthly recurring revenue = active full-price subscriptions × monthly price + active pause subscriptions × pause price`
- `Paid months per customer per year = full-price months + pause months, reported separately as well as combined`
- `Gross margin = (subscription revenue − directly attributable service costs) ÷ subscription revenue`
- `CAC payback in paid months = customer acquisition cost ÷ monthly gross profit per new paid instructor`
- `Activation rate = eligible signups that complete the activation event ÷ eligible signups`
- `Human-free activation rate = activated instructors receiving no human onboarding ÷ activated instructors`
- `Seasonal reactivation rate = eligible paused or seasonally cancelled instructors who resume next season ÷ eligible paused or seasonally cancelled instructors`

Founder research time and assisted-beta learning should be recorded separately from scalable support. Excluding them from normal support metrics must not conceal a product that still depends on them.

## 15. Experience strategy

### 15.1 Instructor experience

The instructor-facing acquisition and activation experience should feel like calm utility: direct, credible, clear about effort and price, and oriented to producing the first real roadmap. It should avoid enterprise language, bespoke-service signals, unexplained jargon, and decorative complexity.

### 15.2 Golfer experience

The golfer-facing roadmap should feel like a quiet editorial performance portfolio: personal, evidence-aware, coach-authored, easy to scan, and focused on a coherent sequence rather than a data dump.

### 15.3 Ethical sales design

The roadmap may make a commercial recommendation, but it must:

- identify the coach as the author of judgment;
- connect the recommendation to the golfer's stated goal and observed barriers;
- distinguish observation, interpretation, plan, and expected evidence;
- avoid guaranteed performance or unsupported certainty;
- show an unpressured next action;
- avoid fake scarcity, countdowns, disguised advertising, or manipulative defaults;
- allow the golfer to ask questions or decline; and
- make edits, corrections, access expectations, and sharing context understandable.

### 15.4 Accessibility and responsive quality

Acquisition, signup, authoring, preview, sharing, and golfer viewing all require accessible content structure, keyboard and focus behavior, readable contrast and type, meaningful labels, usable error recovery, and coherent narrow-screen layouts. Exact acceptance criteria belong in the design and prototype specifications and require verification against the applicable standard and implementation.

## 16. Validation program

Validation must separate four stages:

1. **Assisted research** — founder-led interviews, concept exploration, and workflow observation. This can identify problems but cannot prove self-service.
2. **Assisted beta** — real instructors and golfers use the offer while founder help is allowed and measured. This can expose missing structure and risk but cannot count as unassisted activation evidence.
3. **True self-serve activation** — instructors encounter the explanation, sign up, create, preview, and share without scheduled human onboarding. Reactive support is logged. This tests the actual model.
4. **Scaled self-serve acquisition** — channel, conversion, retention, seasonality, support, and payback are tested across broader cohorts only after the core activation works.

### 16.1 Questions the program must answer

1. Can an instructor understand the value without a live explanation?
2. Can the instructor sign up without help?
3. Can the instructor create the first roadmap without help?
4. Can standardized templates still produce a personal, credible result?
5. Will the instructor share it with a real golfer?
6. Does the golfer understand the plan and package recommendation?
7. Does the experience improve package confidence or conversion without pressure?
8. Will the instructor pay **[PRICING HYPOTHESIS — REQUIRES VALIDATION] approximately CAD $75 per month**?
9. Will the instructor remain subscribed after the first package opportunity?
10. How does seasonality affect cancellation, pause, and paid months?
11. Do paused or cancelled instructors return the next season?
12. Can acquisition and support remain economical at the price?

### 16.2 Recommended sequence

`[SIMULATED OWNER DECISION — REQUIRES AARON REVIEW]`

- Conduct Canada-wide discovery with 12 instructors: 10 matching the solo core and 2 team/facility boundary cases.
- Conduct 12 golfer interviews focused on plan comprehension, decision confidence, trust, and existing post-assessment experiences.
- Test the acquisition explanation and activation prototype with 8 previously unexposed qualified instructors; do not teach them the proposition first.
- Retain golfer-roadmap comprehension testing with 10 golfers and authenticity review with 5 instructors.
- Run an assisted beta with 5–8 instructors, explicitly measuring every founder intervention.
- If code is later authorized through the design gate, run a true self-serve beta with at least 15 qualified instructors, reactive support only, real share behavior, and actual response to the working price.
- Consider broader product-led acquisition only after activation, support, trust, and commercial signals pass agreed thresholds.

Participant counts are planning recommendations, not claims of statistical representativeness.

### 16.3 Go/no-go logic

Proceed only when evidence supports all three layers:

- **Desirability:** instructors and golfers experience the problem and understand the value.
- **Usability and trust:** qualified instructors activate without onboarding, golfers comprehend the plan, and guardrails hold.
- **Commercial viability:** real payment, retention, paid-month, acquisition, support, and reactivation evidence can plausibly support the low-price model.

Pause, narrow, or stop when the offer needs recurring founder explanation, custom work, high support, pressure tactics, or uneconomical acquisition to function. Do not rescue a failed self-serve test by quietly turning it back into a managed service.

## 18. Phased product and business path

### Phase A — problem and message evidence

Validate the initial customer, package-sale problem, language, sample-roadmap credibility, price comprehension, and seasonal context.

### Phase B — non-coded acquisition and activation design

Design and test the landing page, sample output, pricing, FAQ, signup, coach setup, package setup, first roadmap, preview, share, and return state, alongside the golfer-facing roadmap.

### Phase C — assisted beta learning

Use a manual or non-coded service only to learn content, workflow, trust, and commercial behavior. Label and measure assistance. Do not present this stage as proof of scalable self-service.

Only after explicit design-gate approval, test the actual product with qualified solo instructors, real shares, real price response, low reactive support, and seasonality tracking.

### Phase E — product-led acquisition and retention

Expand acquisition experiments only after the core loop is usable and credible. Improve activation, recurring use, renewal and referral deliverables, cancellation, pause, and reactivation before considering team features.

### Phase F — later team tier

Explore small academy and multi-coach needs if solo-instructor evidence and observed demand justify the added complexity. Keep it self-serve by default.
